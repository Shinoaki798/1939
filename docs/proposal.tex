\section*{Introduction}
A language model's knowledge cutoff is usually described as a wall. I suspect
it is a \textbf{membrane with structure}:
some post-cutoff concepts are unreachable, others are assembled from parts the
model already has and are half predictable. To characterise it I will train
\textbf{one Transformer network} from random initialisation on newspaper text
ending before the Second World War --- American and German press, the German in
the original, because the country that started the war kept a record the
American press could not --- and use that one model to answer three questions. \textbf{RQ1 (permeability):} which classes of post-1939 concepts can
a pre-1939 model reach? \textbf{RQ2 (invertibility):} can the boundary be inverted
into a tool that dates a passage and audits a corpus for post-1939 text? \textbf{RQ3 (foresight by perspective):} how much
of 1939--45 was already implied by the press of mid-1939 --- American or
German? Beyond history, this bounds what ``held-out'' tests test and whether
contamination is detectable. Deep learning is the
instrument: these quantities exist only in a trained model's next-token
distribution, so the project \emph{is} the training, validation and testing of
that model. Training ends \textbf{30 June 1939}; July--August 1939 is an
\textbf{embargo}, excluded from training and scored evaluation.

\section*{Background \& Related Work}
Language models degrade on text written after their training window
\citep{lazaridou2021}; temporal conditioning improves future-fact calibration
\citep{dhingra2022}; Time Machine GPT \citep{drinkall2024} trains
point-in-time models. Recent from-scratch models with 19th- and 20th-century cutoffs,
including 1939 \citep{timecapsule2025,ranke2025,talkie2026}, aim to
\emph{produce} such models; this project studies the boundary itself.
SemEval-2020 Task~1 \citep{schlechtweg2020}, HistBERT \citep{qiu2022} and
19th-century models \citep{hosseini2021} measure how \emph{existing} words drift
with masked encoders; RQ1 asks which \emph{not-yet-existing} concepts a fixed
earlier vocabulary can reach, which needs a causal model with a hard boundary. ChroniclingAmericaQA \citep{piryani2024} shows OCR errors cut accuracy, hence
an OCR covariate.

\section*{Data Processing}
\textbf{Sources.} All text is pre-cutoff, in the original language; nothing
is machine-translated. \emph{English:} American Stories \citep{dell2023},
article-level text from Chronicling America, is the backbone and sole source
of scored sets; training-only additions: Chronicling America pages digitised
since that snapshot (1930s, page OCR) \citep{chronam}, the Congressional Record
\citep{congrecord}, the Federal Register \citep{fedreg} and case law \citep{cap}
(legal text $\le$10\%), and public-domain books \citep{locpdbooks} ($\le$12\%).
\emph{German:} newspapers from Europeana \citep{europeana2015} and the
Deutsches Zeitungsportal \citep{ddb} (38 titles in 1933--39), deduplicated
by title and date, plus the 1925 and 1930 issues of the NSDAP daily
\emph{V\"olkischer Beobachter} \citep{vb}; German is $\le$25\% of seen
tokens per period. Every file is pinned by revision and checksum.
\textbf{Recency weighting.} The pool centres on the 1910s but the model must
live in 1939: the 1930s are seen twice; the 1920s fill the remainder ($\le$35\%
of seen tokens); pre-1920 $\le$8\%; within a period, sampling weight
$\propto \exp(-(1939-\text{year})/5)$. \textbf{Splits} by publication date,
never shuffled: a fixed 2\% of every year 1900--1955, per language, is held out
and never trained on; \textsc{Train} $=$ the rest $\le$1939-06-30; \textsc{Val}
$=$ a disjoint 2\% $\le$1939-06-30; \textsc{Test-A/B/C} $=$ held-out 1930--38 / 1939-09--45 /
1946--55. \textbf{Cleaning}, in order: (1)~language filter (stopword vote per
article and title-year); (2)~MinHash dedup of reprints (word 5-grams, Jaccard
$\ge$0.8) before splitting; (3)~OCR quality $=$ period-lexicon hit rate per
language, cut at a threshold set from its histogram; (4)~documents containing
a C1 coinage (English or German list) are dropped whole.
\textbf{Format:} 48k BPE trained only on native pre-cutoff English and German;
documents joined by an end-of-text token into 2048-token sequences; numbers are
bits-per-byte. \textbf{RQ1 probe set:} $\sim$200
post-cutoff terms in four classes --- C1 coinage (\textit{radar}), C2
compositional (\textit{atomic bomb}), C3 old word, new sense (\textit{blitz}),
C4 proper noun, new association (\textit{Hiroshima}) --- each with a
frequency-matched control in $\ge$5 sentence contexts, frozen in advance. \textbf{RQ2
calibration:} held-out pre-cutoff corpora with a known share $\varepsilon$
(0.1--50\%) of 1939-09--45 articles mixed in. \textbf{RQ3 propositions:}
$\sim$60 binary propositions about 1939--45, each with same-topic US (English)
and German (German) contexts from June and late August 1939 (embargo text as
conditioning only); continuations are English.

\section*{Architecture}
One \textbf{decoder-only Transformer} as in the course: token embeddings,
stacked blocks of multi-head self-attention and position-wise feed-forward
layers, residual connections, layer normalisation, softmax over the vocabulary,
with attention \textbf{causally masked} (position $i$ sees $\le i$), a
next-token predictor. Decoder-only because every measurement is a sequence
probability of unseen text: a masked encoder's pseudo-likelihoods give none, and
an encoder--decoder has no source sequence. From scratch in PyTorch, no
pretrained weights, \textbf{no second Transformer}: all controls are built at
evaluation on this one model.
\textbf{Size:} 350M parameters (24 layers, $d{=}1024$, 16 heads, 48k vocab) on
up to 10B seen tokens ($\approx$29/parameter); no text is seen more than twice,
as repetition raises memorisation and biases surprisal. Compute
$\approx$2.1$\times$10$^{19}$ FLOPs, $\sim$85\,h on the RTX~5080 (H100: 15\,h),
before the midterm.
\textbf{Measurement.} For term class $c$, $\Delta_c = \mathbb{E}_{t\in
\mathcal{T}_c}[s(t)] - \mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{C}_c}[s(t)]$, $s$ the
surprisal in fixed contexts, $\mathcal{C}_c$ matched controls. RQ1 is the
profile $(\Delta_{C1..C4})$; RQ2 fits a monotone map to $\varepsilon$,
reporting the minimum detectable $\varepsilon$; RQ3 scores
forced-choice continuations by log-likelihood ratio as Brier score per
perspective; all with bootstrap 95\% CIs.
\textbf{Success:} $\Delta_{C2}$ significantly smallest; a step at 1939, not a
slope; $\hat\varepsilon$ monotone; perspectives above the $n$-gram floor and
significantly different.

\section*{Baseline Model}
Following the course progression, with the same split, tokenizer and budget:
a \textbf{vanilla RNN}, an \textbf{LSTM} and a \textbf{GRU} language model (2
layers, $\sim$50M parameters, next-token objective), which justify the
Transformer as primary model. A \textbf{modified Kneser--Ney 5-gram} (KenLM defaults) is the
non-neural floor; it cannot compose unseen compounds, so it should show no
C1/C2 ordering.
\textbf{Plan} (individual): corpus frozen 20 Oct; baselines 17--21 Oct;
Transformer 22 Oct--1 Nov; then evaluation.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
Pre-1939 newspapers carry the racial, ethnic and gender language of their time;
the model reproduces it, which is documented, not sanitised. German press after
1933 was state-controlled, so the ``German perspective'' is what Germans were
permitted to read; RQ3 results describe a record, not nations, and corpus
shares by source are reported. In collection, OCR noise concentrates in older and German
(Fraktur) text, and year-only book dates can mislead (hence the C1 screen). All sources are public domain or openly licensed, and attributed; as the papers
name private people, the model is not released for querying them; the RQ2 tool,
misusable as an accusation, ships with its detection limit.
